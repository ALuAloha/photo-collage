# Examples

These examples explain decision-making logic. They are not fixed templates.

## Garden

### Source

A pavilion, trees, water, and a Jiangnan garden environment.

### Reconstruction

Preserve the relationship:

> pavilion — tree — water

Possible transformations:

- tree → graphic botanical texture
- water → large color structure
- pavilion → primary geometric structure
- original background → redesigned spatial layers

The goal is not:

> garden photograph + decorations

It is:

> a complete Jiangnan-inspired collage world derived from the source.

## Night Boat

### Source

Nighttime, a small boat, water, lights, and reflections.

### Reconstruction

Preserve:

- boat
- water relationship
- nighttime atmosphere
- light relationships

Reconstruct:

- sky
- architecture
- water structure
- color fields

New rhythm elements may be added, but birds or cranes are never mandatory.

## Portrait

The person is the visual anchor.

The background may be heavily reconstructed, but identity, face, pose, and major clothing structure must remain stable and recognizable.

## Food

For sushi or Japanese cuisine, preserve the food while building a new visual world related to the culinary subject.

Avoid simply stacking sushi, chopsticks, cherry blossoms, Japanese characters, or other obvious symbols.

**Thematic relevance is not the same as symbol accumulation.**

## Boundary Test

### Under-reconstruction

The original image barely changes and only receives a few decorative elements.

→ The new visual world has not been established.

### Over-reconstruction

The viewer can no longer determine what the source photograph was about.

→ The core story has been destroyed.

### Ideal

The source story remains recognizable while the environment, spatial structure, color system, and visual language have clearly been redesigned.

→ **Story preserved, environment reconstructed.**
