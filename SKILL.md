# Photo Collage World

Transform a real photograph into a complete, theme-driven collage world.

This is not a filter, a decorative overlay, or a fixed collage template.

The core philosophy is:

> Preserve the story, not the environment.
> Preserve the relationships, not every object.

The original photograph provides the visual DNA. The surrounding world may be substantially redesigned.

## 1. Core Creative Principle

Follow this hierarchy:

**Original Image → Core Subject → Visual DNA → Thematic World → Color System → Composition → Controlled Creative Details**

The final image must remain recognizably connected to the source photograph.

Do not treat the task as:

> "Keep the original photo and add decorations."

Instead:

> "Extract the visual identity of the original photograph and rebuild a new visual world around it."

The result should feel intentionally designed, visually rich, and editorial while retaining the identity of the original photograph.

## 2. Identify the Core Subject First

Before designing the new world, determine what the viewer should remember from the original image.

Identify:

- the primary person, object, animal, food, architecture, or scene
- distinctive shapes and silhouettes
- recognizable colors
- important textures and materials
- cultural or geographic cues
- the main visual action or relationship
- the emotional atmosphere

The core subject must remain the visual anchor.

Preserve the subject's:

- identity
- recognizability
- essential silhouette
- important structure
- defining visual characteristics
- primary action or pose when applicable
- important relationships with other core subjects

Creative transformation should happen primarily around the subject, not by replacing or substantially redesigning it.

Do not let decorative elements overwhelm, hide, fragment, or visually replace the original subject.

## 3. Extract the Visual DNA

Do not assume every visible object must be preserved.

Extract the photograph's **Visual DNA**, which may include:

- dominant and secondary colors
- distinctive shapes and silhouettes
- textures and materials
- patterns
- cultural references
- geographic characteristics
- architectural language
- natural elements
- lighting
- atmosphere
- spatial relationships
- emotional tone

Some objects may disappear if they are not important to the image's identity.

A small detail may become an important creative motif if it carries strong visual or cultural meaning.

**Preserve what makes the photograph recognizable, not everything that happens to be visible.**

## 4. Build a Thematic Visual World

Create a new surrounding world based directly on the subject and Visual DNA.

Every major creative element should have a conceptual reason to exist.

Use the relationship:

**Original subject → visual association → thematic element**

Avoid:

**Original subject → random beautiful decoration**

Examples of possible directions:

- A Suzhou scene may inspire Jiangnan architecture, gardens, water, lanterns, silk, embroidery, fans, or related visual language.
- A food photograph may inspire ingredients, utensils, patterns, packaging, culinary materials, or abstract forms derived from the food.
- A portrait may inspire a world derived from clothing, location, culture, profession, mood, or visible objects.
- A natural scene may inspire an expanded ecosystem based on its colors, forms, atmosphere, and environmental characteristics.

These are creative directions, not fixed motifs.

## 5. Do Not Imitate a Fixed Template

Reference images may inform:

- visual language
- color relationships
- scale contrast
- negative space
- layering
- visual rhythm
- degree of abstraction
- editorial atmosphere

A reference image is **not a template**.

Do not mechanically reproduce the same:

- cranes
- birds
- lanterns
- architecture
- central lines
- framing
- arrangement
- decorative objects
- color palette

Use the visual logic, not the visual inventory.

New elements must be independently derived from the source photograph.

## 6. Color Design

Color is part of the composition, not a final filter.

Analyze:

- dominant hue
- secondary colors
- accent colors
- warm/cool relationships
- brightness
- saturation
- tonal contrast

Then build a deliberate color system.

Use strong color contrast, controlled accents, large unified color areas, warm/cool contrast, or light/dark contrast when appropriate.

A single distinctive color area may become a major visual anchor.

**Color must support the subject, not compete with it.**

Do not darken or recolor the core subject merely to achieve a dramatic graphic effect.

## 7. Composition

Build the composition using:

- large, medium, and small elements
- negative space
- scale contrast
- visual rhythm
- overlap
- cropping
- asymmetry
- directional movement
- foreground/background separation
- controlled visual tension

Use a clear hierarchy:

**Primary Subject → Secondary Structure → Supporting Details**

Unconventional composition may be used when it improves the image:

- elements extending beyond the frame
- oversized secondary objects
- unexpected scale relationships
- partial cropping
- floating structures
- controlled visual imbalance

These are optional techniques.

Do not force a fixed central line, symmetry, framing, or layout into every image.

## 8. Element Density and Visual Hierarchy

A rich collage does not mean random visual clutter.

Use a deliberate distribution of:

- dominant elements
- secondary elements
- small accents
- quiet areas
- negative space

Supporting elements may create a richer visual world when they remain subordinate to the main subject.

Avoid both extremes:

**Too empty:** original photo plus a few decorations.

**Too dense:** the original subject becomes difficult to recognize.

The ideal result is a rich but controlled visual world.

## 9. Preserve the Original Story

The final image may change substantially in appearance, but the underlying visual story should remain.

Ask:

> If someone saw the original photograph and the final artwork side by side, would they understand why the final artwork came from this photograph?

The answer should be yes.

Preserve important relationships such as:

- person ↔ object
- person ↔ environment
- food ↔ table
- architecture ↔ location
- subject ↔ cultural context
- subject ↔ distinctive color
- subject ↔ recognizable action

The environment may be transformed. Core relationships should not be arbitrarily destroyed.

## 10. Controlled Secondary Creation

AI may introduce new visual ideas when they improve the artwork.

Secondary creation may include:

- new structures
- abstract forms
- symbolic objects
- visual metaphors
- unexpected scale
- floating elements
- graphic shapes
- surreal spatial relationships

Secondary creation must remain subordinate to the original subject.

Use creativity to expand the world, not erase the source.

When the source is visually ambiguous, creative interpretation may become more important, but the final result should still retain meaningful evidence from the source.

## 11. Realism and Stylization

The final result does not need to remain photorealistic.

It may combine:

- photography
- collage
- illustration
- graphic design
- paper-like textures
- line art
- architectural forms
- surreal spatial construction
- editorial art direction

However, the original core subject should remain convincing and recognizable unless the source itself is highly abstract.

Do not make everything look like generic AI art.

Create a sophisticated visual world that clearly originates from a real photograph.

## 12. Common Failure Modes

Avoid:

### Subject Disappearance
Do not let the creative background become more important than the original subject.

### Template Copying
Do not reproduce reference motifs regardless of the source photograph.

### Random Decoration
Do not add beautiful elements without a conceptual connection to the source.

### Excessive Transformation
Do not transform the photograph so aggressively that its source relationship becomes unrecognizable.

### Excessive Literal Preservation
Do not preserve every original object simply because it exists.

### Overcrowding
Do not fill every empty area with decorative elements.

### Forced Composition
Do not impose a fixed central line, symmetry, or recurring layout.

### Subject Darkening
Do not make the core subject excessively dark or low-contrast merely for drama.

### Generic AI Aesthetic
Avoid generic AI posters, travel advertisements, or fantasy illustrations.

The final work should feel specifically derived from the original photograph.

## 13. Execution Workflow

### Step 1 — Analyze
Identify the primary subject, secondary subjects, relationships, colors, atmosphere, and contextual clues.

### Step 2 — Extract
Determine the Visual DNA.

### Step 3 — Prioritize
Separate essential visual information, useful supporting information, and disposable environmental information.

### Step 4 — Conceptualize
Develop a thematic world directly related to the source.

### Step 5 — Design
Establish color, scale, hierarchy, negative space, and composition.

### Step 6 — Create
Transform the surrounding environment while preserving the core subject.

### Step 7 — Balance
Check subject prominence, element density, color contrast, and visual hierarchy.

### Step 8 — Validate
Check:

1. Is the original subject still clearly recognizable?
2. Does the new world logically originate from the original image?
3. Are major creative elements thematically relevant?
4. Does the composition have clear hierarchy?
5. Is the image rich without becoming cluttered?
6. Does it feel independently designed rather than copied?
7. Is the transformation creative without destroying the original story?

If any answer is no, revise before finalizing.

## 14. Final Quality Standard

The ideal result should be:

**Original Subject**
- clear
- recognizable
- visually dominant
- structurally respected

**Creative World**
- thematically connected
- visually rich
- independently designed
- not a template copy

**Color**
- intentional
- distinctive
- harmonious or deliberately contrasting
- supportive of the subject

**Composition**
- strong hierarchy
- meaningful negative space
- controlled density
- optional unconventional framing

**Overall Impression**
- creative
- editorial
- memorable
- visually sophisticated
- clearly derived from the original photograph

The final image should make the viewer think:

> **This is still that photograph — but someone has turned its visual DNA into an entire world.**
